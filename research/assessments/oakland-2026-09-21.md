## Why this entity was assessed

The scanner flagged the 10 September 2026 unsealing of a federal indictment against Oakland Police Department officers on FAA-fraud conspiracy charges, days before a federal oversight hearing. Oakland has never been assessed before (`last_assessed: null`), so this is a first-ever full baseline resting on the public record, not only on in-window evidence.

## The attribution question, resolved before anything moved

The brief for tonight was to establish whether this conduct is the officers' private fraud or a departmental failure. It is closer to the former, and three specific findings settle it.

**1. The scanner's framing is wrong on a material fact: one of the three defendants is not a city employee.** The scan says "three Oakland cops indicted". In fact **William Miller was an FAA aviation safety inspector** — a federal official — described in reporting as "the linchpin in the plot who made it possible for the officers to fake flights". Only Brandon Mart and David Mac are OPD employees, both pilots in the department's Air Support Unit. Recorded as a scanner correction.

**2. The conduct is individual credential fraud, not departmental policy.** Mart and Mac "allegedly conspired with a Federal Aviation Administration inspector to obtain certifications between July 2023 and March 2024", submitting fraudulent documents for at least four checkrides. The benefit ran to the two officers personally — three fraudulent certifications for Mart, one for Mac. No allegation was located that OPD directed, knew of, or condoned the scheme.

**3. It is a procedural event on conduct that predates this window by two and a half years.** The indictment was unsealed on 10 September 2026; Mart and Mac pleaded not guilty. The alleged scheme ran July 2023 to March 2024. Nothing has been decided.

**Where the city's responsibility genuinely does attach — and it does — is thinner than it first appears.** These were city employees, flying the city's police helicopter over a populated city, on credentials that were allegedly faked. That is a real public-safety exposure created by city staff in the course of city work, and it raises a legitimate question about whether OPD verified its own pilots' credentials. **No evidence either way was located on that question.** It is scored as an unresolved supervision gap in AB4 Systemic Learning and I3 Internal Consistency, both at 2 of 5, and it is not treated as an established departmental failure.

**Why this differs from the LAHSA case.** There, the harm belonged to a separate legal entity and nothing attached to the city at all. Here the actors are the city's own officers, so attribution is arguable rather than excluded. The result is a scored supervision concern, not a scored institutional harm.

## The countervailing fact the scan did not carry

Oakland's police department is two weeks from leaving 23 years of federal court oversight. Federal monitor Robert Warshaw reported in May 2026 that "For the first time in the history of its federal court oversight, the Oakland Police Department has complied with all of its 51 mandated tasks" — covering use of force, discipline, supervision, training and internal affairs. Judge William H. Orrick III said he would terminate the monitorship and close the 2003 case on 29 September 2026 if compliance holds.

That is independently verified accountability progress from a tier-5 source, and this baseline credits it at AB2 Correction Willingness 3/5 and AB3 Transparency 3/5. It is not credited higher for two reasons: it took 23 years under court compulsion, and the 29 September decision has not been made. A separate grand-theft charge against an OPD sergeant on 28 August 2026, plus the 10 September indictments, are exactly the facts that cut against "sustained compliance".

## Key Findings

- Why it matters: the scanner said three Oakland cops were indicted. One of the three was a federal FAA inspector, not a city employee. Only two of the accused work for Oakland, and both are police helicopter pilots.
- Why it matters: the alleged fraud was the pilots' own, not the department's. Prosecutors say Brandon Mart and David Mac faked flight tests between July 2023 and March 2024 to win credentials they had not earned. No one has alleged the department directed or knew about it, so Oakland's score does not fall for it.
- Why it matters: there is still a real city question inside it. Two city employees allegedly flew the city's police helicopter on faked certifications. Nothing public shows whether Oakland ever checked its own pilots' paperwork, so that counts as an open supervision gap.
- Why it matters: Oakland's police force is two weeks from leaving 23 years of federal court supervision. A federal monitor found in May 2026 that the department met all 51 required reforms for the first time ever. A judge decides on 29 September 2026 whether to close the case.
- Why it matters: Oakland's homelessness numbers fell but the reason is disputed. The city counted 4,410 unhoused people in 2026, down from 5,485 in 2024. The count came right after the biggest encampment clearance in city history, and shelter beds did not really grow.
- Why it matters: the plan costs more than three times what the city has. Oakland's homelessness strategy needs about $406 million a year and has about $122 million — a gap of $284.3 million.
