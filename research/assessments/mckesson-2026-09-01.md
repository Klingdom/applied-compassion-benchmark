## Why this entity was assessed

The scanner flagged McKesson on the ShinyHunters extortion group's claim of theft of roughly 284 million patient data records, and on McKesson's disclosure of a cyber incident. McKesson has never been individually assessed (rotation-state `last_assessed: null`); the published 31.3 is a carried-forward seed. Its priority score of 75 was the second-highest in the cycle.

**This entity carried an explicit gate warning:** a superlative claim on partly undated sourcing, matching the pattern behind the confirmed 2026-07-24 defect.

## Evidence-date, lifecycle and attribution checks

**GATE WARNING 1 RESOLVED — THE SUPERLATIVE CLAIM DOES NOT SURVIVE CONTACT WITH A PRIMARY SOURCE, AND DOES NOT MOVE THE SCORE.**

The scanner reported "theft of roughly 284 million patient data records". The figure is wrong in two ways, both confirmed against primary and near-primary sources:

1. **It is an attacker's claim, not a confirmed finding.** ShinyHunters asserted it. McKesson has confirmed no figure. TechCrunch quotes the hackers themselves as "unsure of how many individuals are ultimately affected".
2. **It counts database rows, not people.** BleepingComputer quotes the group's own clarification: the number is "a raw count of approximately 284 million data records, or lines, rather than a count of unique individuals." Describing it as "284 million patients" would overstate the harm by an unknown and possibly very large factor. **No patient-count figure is used anywhere in this assessment.**

**REGULATORY FILING LOCATED — the gate warning's instruction was followed and it produced the decisive source.** McKesson filed a Form 8-K with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, event date 25 August 2026, filed 28 August 2026: "On August 25, 2026, McKesson Corporation discovered a cybersecurity incident affecting its information systems." This is a tier-5 primary document and is the anchor for the conduct scored here.

**DATE CORRECTION.** The scanner recorded `evidence_date: 2026-08-31` and stated McKesson "confirmed and disclosed the cyber incident on 31 August 2026". **That is three days late.** McKesson disclosed publicly by SEC filing on **28 August 2026**. 31 August is the TechCrunch publication date. The scanner also marked the BleepingComputer URL `date_verified: false`; on re-fetch it resolves and is dated **28 August 2026**. Both corrections matter, because the speed of disclosure is precisely what is being scored.

**FILING-FORM FINDING.** The disclosure was made under **Item 7.01 (Regulation FD Disclosure)**, which is voluntary, not under **Item 1.05**, which covers material cybersecurity incidents. The filing states the company "has not determined that the incident is material". So McKesson disclosed faster than it was required to, and simultaneously disclaimed the trigger that would have required it. Both facts are scored — the first favourably under Accountability, the second unfavourably under Empathy and Validation.

**ATTRIBUTION.** Per the cycle instruction, the breach's existence is not scored against McKesson. What is scored is **detection, disclosure and remediation conduct**: discovery on the same day exfiltration completed; incident-response activation; external experts engaged; public filing within three days; and — on the other side — no reference in the filing to notifying affected individuals, and a materiality disclaimer. The criminal act is the attacker's.

**Lifecycle re-check.** All four sources (SEC EDGAR, BleepingComputer, TechCrunch, Help Net Security) were re-fetched or re-searched on the assessment date. All resolve. None has been amended or retracted.

## Key Findings

- The headline number is not what it appears. ShinyHunters claimed 284 million records from McKesson, but the group itself described the figure as "a raw count of approximately 284 million data records, or lines, rather than a count of unique individuals." McKesson has confirmed no patient count. The benchmark does not use the figure.
- McKesson disclosed faster than the rules required. It found the incident on 25 August 2026 and filed publicly with the SEC on 28 August 2026 — three days. It filed under the voluntary Regulation FD item, not the mandatory material-incident item.
- In the same filing McKesson said it "has not determined that the incident is material." Disclosing quickly while disclaiming materiality is a mixed signal, and it is scored both ways.
- The SEC filing says nothing about telling affected people. It directs stakeholders to the company website. Investor disclosure moved in three days; patient notification is not evidenced at all.
- The assessed score of 30.6 is 0.7 points from the published 31.3, in the same Developing band. A breach that a criminal group described in record-breaking terms produced no score movement, because the conduct actually documented — fast detection, fast voluntary disclosure, no patient notification — roughly cancels out.

## Strongest Dimensions

**Accountability (2.8)**. Three distinct pieces of evidence: a voluntary public filing three days after discovery of a serious intrusion; an independent-monitor compliance regime with periodic auditing and stipulated penalties arising from the 2017 Department of Justice settlement; and an opioid settlement structured so that at least 85% of $7.9 billion goes to remediation rather than to general revenue. None of these is exemplary, but all three are real, documented and above the anchor-2 floor.

## Weakest Dimensions

**Awareness (2.0)**, **Empathy (2.0)** and **Integrity (2.0)**. McKesson's customers are healthcare providers, not patients, and no mechanism was located by which the company detects, hears from or validates the people whose records it holds. The patient data at issue sat in third-party applications. When it was taken, the company's first public act was a filing addressed to investors.

## Evidence Gaps

- No evidence located on whether or when McKesson notified affected individuals. The 8-K does not mention it. This is an absence, and per the scoring convention it is scored 2, not 1 — absence of disclosure is not evidence of harm.
- No independently verified count of affected people exists. The only figure in circulation is the attacker's, and the attacker disclaimed it as a row count.
- No in-window employee testimony or independent workplace assessment was located, so Integrity subdimension I3 (internal consistency) rests on absence and is scored 2.
- The FY2026 Impact Report is self-published corporate material. It is used only as weak (tier 2) evidence of stated commitments and is excluded from any band-crossing test.

## Disclosure — what this score measures

Several subdimensions here are scored on **absence of disclosure, not on evidence of harm**. Specifically: A2, A3, A4 (contextual sensitivity, blind-spot mitigation, signal amplification), E1–E5 apart from E4, B1, B2, B4, B5, S1–S4 and I2, I3, I5. In each of these McKesson has published nothing that would let an outsider score it higher, and nothing adverse was found either. Per the standing convention these are scored **2**, never 1. A score of 1 would require positive documented evidence of a specific failure, and none exists for those subdimensions.

**16 of 40 subdimensions rest on absence of disclosure.** That is the single most important caveat on this score. McKesson is not being marked down for misconduct in those areas; it is being marked at the low anchor because it has said nothing an assessor can verify.

The subdimensions that do rest on positive evidence — AB1, AB2, AB3, AB5, EQ5, AC1, AC4, B3, S5 — are scored 3, above the absence floor, on the strength of the SEC filing, the Department of Justice monitor regime and the opioid remediation structure.

## Recommendation

**Confirm. No change proposal.** The assessed composite is 30.6 against a published 31.3 — a delta of -0.7, well inside the 5.0 filing threshold, with no band crossing. Neither filing trigger is met.

This is the correct outcome and it is worth stating why. A criminal group asserted a record-breaking theft; the number turned out to be an unverified row count that the attacker itself would not stand behind; and the company's own documented conduct was faster and more forthcoming than the sector norm on the investor side, and silent on the patient side. Scoring a large downgrade off the attacker's press release would have repeated the exact defect this cycle was warned about.

**Watch flag:** re-assess if and when McKesson determines the incident material, files under Item 1.05, notifies affected individuals, or a regulator (HHS Office for Civil Rights, state attorneys general) opens a public action. Any of those would produce real conduct evidence in a currently empty area.
