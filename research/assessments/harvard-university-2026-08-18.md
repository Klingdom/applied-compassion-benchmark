### Analysis

Two forces roughly cancel. Upward: Harvard bore a documented, very large financial cost — officials put federal actions at up to $1 billion a year — rather than accept the administration's demands, and a federal court has now vindicated that position. Downward: Harvard's treatment of its own staff and graduate workers has deteriorated over the same period. Layoffs have hit the Kennedy School, the School of Public Health, the Medical School and roughly 40 people at the engineering school; the Faculty of Arts and Sciences carries a projected $365 million structural deficit; the hiring freeze was extended; more than 3,600 people signed a petition arguing the layoffs lacked concrete financial justification; 4,000 graduate workers struck in April 2026; and Harvard removed about 800 graduate students from their bargaining unit. Boundaries (2.80) and Integrity's internal-consistency subdimension carry that finding.

The net effect is a confirmation, not a change.

## Key Findings

- Why it matters: Harvard refused to settle and won. A federal judge dismissed the Trump administration's antisemitism suit on 13 August 2026, finding the alleged incidents "too isolated and episodic" to show an ongoing Title VI violation. Harvard held that position while its own officials estimated federal actions could cost it $1 billion a year.
- Why it matters: the same institution treats outsiders and insiders differently. Harvard scores 3.80 of 5 on Equity, its best dimension, but 2.80 of 5 on Boundaries — its worst — because staff and graduate workers absorbed the cost of the budget squeeze.
- Harvard removed roughly 800 graduate students from their union by reclassifying them as non-employees. About 4,000 graduate workers struck in April 2026. More than 3,600 people signed a petition saying the layoffs lacked concrete financial justification.
- Financial aid moved the other way. Harvard College is now free for families earning $100,000 or less and tuition-free for families earning $200,000 or less — a genuine, costly widening of access held in place during a budget crisis.
- The score barely moves. Assessed 53.8 against a published 52.3 — a 1.5-point difference, well inside the noise of a desk assessment.

## Strongest Dimensions

Equity (3.80 of 5). The income-based free-tuition thresholds, the Harvard and the Legacy of Slavery programme, and disaggregated climate reporting together clear the "documented reduction in gaps" anchor for EQ1 and EQ2.

## Weakest Dimensions

Boundaries (2.80 of 5) and Empathy (2.80 of 5). Self-sustainability scores 2 of 5: frontline and administrative staff carried layoffs, a hiring freeze and suspended raises while the endowment stood at $57 billion. ## Evidence Gaps

Layoff totals are reported school-by-school with no university-wide figure. No independent audit of the layoff decision process exists. Where disclosure is simply absent rather than negative, the low anchor was applied and is stated as such.
