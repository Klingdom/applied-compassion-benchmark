## Why this entity was assessed tonight

The scanner flagged Human Rights Watch reporting of 4 August 2026 that the National Commission on Indigenous Peoples' revised free, prior and informed consent guidelines threaten Indigenous communities' rights. This is distinct from the 27 July SONA protest arrests already assessed on 2026-08-01, but the Philippines has been flagged repeatedly, so the compounding screen applies.

## Scanner date / attribution corrections

- **Evidence is genuinely new and distinct — the scanner is right about that.** The revised free, prior and informed consent guidelines are a separate matter from the 27 July 2026 SONA protest arrests scored on 2026-08-01. The HRW report is dated 2026-08-04 and its URL date is verified.
- **But the underlying policy act predates the window.** The National Commission on Indigenous Peoples issued the revised guidelines on **1 May 2026**, three months before this cycle opened. What is in-window is HRW's first documentation of them. The guidelines remain in force, so the finding is assessable, but its in-window character is documentary rather than a fresh state act.
- **COMPOUNDING SCREEN APPLIED — this is why no proposal is filed.** The assessed composite of 27.5 is 5.3 points below the published 32.8, which would clear the threshold. But 3.4 of those points were already recorded on 2026-08-01, when the Philippines was assessed at 29.4 and **confirmed** on the SONA arrests. The increment genuinely attributable to tonight's new evidence is roughly **1.9 points**. Adding a previously-confirmed delta to a small new one in order to cross the 5-point threshold is exactly the compounding the repeat-flag discipline forbids.
- **Single-source limitation.** The finding rests on one tier-3 source (Human Rights Watch). No second independently dated source documenting the revised guidelines was located within the window.

## Key Findings

- The Philippines cut the time Indigenous communities get to say no. Under 2012 rules, communities had up to two months to reach consensus on projects affecting their ancestral land. Revised rules issued on 1 May 2026 give 30 days for large projects and 7 days for small ones.
- Officials no longer have to go and look. The revised guidelines drop the requirement for a field-based investigation. The Commission "may issue a Certificate of Non-Overlap" from documents alone, potentially approving projects that sit on ancestral land.
- And communities lost the ability to complain. Human Rights Watch found the guidelines "remove avenues for redress for Indigenous communities" disputing permits, warning that mining may already be under way before a challenge can be heard.
- The score falls 5.3 points, and the benchmark still did not file a change. About 3.4 of those points were already counted and confirmed on 1 August 2026 for a different event. Only about 1.9 points come from tonight's new evidence — not enough to move a published score.
- One watchdog report is not enough on its own. The finding rests on a single Human Rights Watch source. A second, higher-tier source would convert this watch flag into a formal proposal.
