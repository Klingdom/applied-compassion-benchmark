### Score Difference Analysis

Portugal’s parliament passed a blanket ban on face-concealing clothing in public spaces on 17 July 2026 with a two-thirds majority. Violations carry fines of €200 to €4,000; forcing another person to wear a face veil carries up to three years’ imprisonment. Amnesty International Portugal’s executive director João Godinho Martins said the law "is discriminatory and is a threat to human rights", and Amnesty argues that although explicit references to Islam were removed from earlier drafts, the text still disproportionately affects Muslim women who cover their faces as part of religious practice. The bill originated with the far-right Chega party. This is Portugal’s own legislative conduct, within window, and it moves two dimensions materially: Empathy falls 0.6 raw (Non-Judgment, Perspective-Taking and Cultural Empathy all resolve to anchor 3) and Equity falls 0.3 (Universality and Bias Awareness). Boundaries falls 0.2 on Consent Orientation, because a blanket prohibition removes a choice rather than informing it. The remaining five dimensions move only on the grid, by 0.1 raw each, because the published 3.5 values are not reachable from five integer anchors. The result is a composite difference of exactly -5.0, which reaches the change-proposal threshold. **The band does not change.** Portugal moves from 65.6 to 60.6 and remains Established.

## Key Findings

- **Why it matters: Portugal has made it a fineable offence to cover your face in public.** Parliament passed the ban on 17 July 2026 with a two-thirds majority. Fines run from €200 to €4,000. Forcing someone else to wear a face veil carries up to three years in prison.
- **Why it matters: the country’s own human rights monitor says the law targets Muslim women.** Amnesty International Portugal’s executive director João Godinho Martins said the law "is discriminatory and is a threat to human rights". Amnesty says removing explicit references to Islam from the draft did not change who it falls on.
- **Where the bill came from.** It was originally proposed by the far-right Chega party. The final text was reframed around security and public order, with exceptions, partly to survive constitutional scrutiny under Article 41 of the Portuguese Constitution, which guarantees freedom of religion.
- **Why it matters: Portugal stays in the same band.** The score moves from 65.6 to 60.6 out of 100. Both numbers are Established. The change is flagged for human review rather than applied, because the human rights reading of the law rests on one organisation — Amnesty International — and no court or treaty body has assessed it yet.
- **Portugal ranks 22nd of 193 countries at 65.6 out of 100.** This assessment moves it -5.0 points to 60.6, which reaches the change-proposal threshold. The proposal is filed as pending and is not applied.

## Strongest Dimensions

**Boundaries (3.8).** Portugal communicated the scope of the law, its exceptions and its penalties clearly before it took effect, and preserved the constitutional freedom-of-religion carve-out rather than legislating around it.

## Weakest Dimensions

**Equity (3.0).** A blanket ban that Amnesty says falls disproportionately on Muslim women is, on its face, a universality and bias-awareness problem. **Integrity (3.2)** follows: a law originating with a far-right party and passed over the objection of the country’s principal human rights monitor sits awkwardly against Portugal’s stated values.

## Evidence Gaps

- No estimate of how many women in Portugal actually wear a full-face veil was located, so the practical reach of the law cannot be quantified and EQ1 is scored conservatively.
- No treaty body, Council of Europe or Portuguese court assessment of the law exists yet, which is precisely why a band crossing was not adopted.
- No evidence was found of consultation with affected Muslim women during the drafting, so E2 and E5 rest on the absence of such evidence.
