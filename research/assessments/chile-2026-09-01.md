## Why this entity was assessed

The scanner flagged Chile on Human Rights Watch's 1 September 2026 call for Chile's Congress to reject a package of eight constitutional amendments submitted to the Senate by President Jose Antonio Kast's government on 18 August 2026. The package would create a new "public security" state of emergency that the president could declare against any perceived threat.

Chile was last assessed on 2026-05-03, roughly seven weeks after the Kast government took office on 11 March 2026. That assessment proposed 65.0 against a published 62.5 and was recorded as a sub-threshold confirmation, resting on pension-reform gender-equity provisions enacted under the previous administration (`research/digests/2026-05-03.md`). The published score therefore carries almost no evidence about the conduct of the current government. This cycle is the first assessment with a substantial in-window record of it.

## Evidence-date, lifecycle and attribution checks

**DATE CHECK — PASS on the scanner's two sources.** The HRW item carries `/news/2026/09/` in its URL and resolves to a page dated 1 September 2026. The El Ciudadano item carries `/09/02/` and is a next-day pickup of the same HRW release. Both are inside the window. The conduct date (submission of the amendments) is 18 August 2026; the reporting date is 1 September 2026. Both are correct and describe different things.

**STALE-SOURCE CONFLATION FOUND AND EXCLUDED — this is a material finding.** While searching for a tier-4-or-above corroborating source, search-engine synthesis returned what appeared to be a 2026 United Nations Special Procedures communication to Chile stating "Unauthorised border crossing cannot be considered a crime", together with specific claims that administrative detention of migrants had been extended from 5 days to 60 days renewable to 180, and that healthcare centres, schools and social security agencies would be required to hand personal data on undocumented migrants to immigration authorities.

**On direct re-fetch, the underlying Global Detention Project pages are dated 1 June 2023 and 12 December 2023, and the UN communication they quote is dated 24 May 2023 — three years outside the window.** The detention figures in the genuine 2023 document are different again (48 hours to 7 days, not 5 days to 60 days). The healthcare/school data-sharing claim appears in neither page. **All of these claims are excluded from this assessment.** None of them scored. This is the same failure pattern as the confirmed 2026-07-24 defect: a prior-period statistic surfacing as current.

**BAND-CROSSING EVIDENCE TEST — FAILED.** This assessment crosses a band boundary (Established to Functional). The filing rule requires at least two independent sources with at least one at tier 4 or above. Chile has three independent, dated, within-period sources — Human Rights Watch (tier 3), NPR (tier 2) and MercoPress (tier 2) — but **no tier-4-or-above source was located** for the 2026 conduct after the 2023 material was excluded. The test is recorded as `passed: false` and the proposal is routed `flag-for-review` rather than asserted as a settled band change.

**ATTRIBUTION.** Everything scored here is the conduct of the Chilean state: a decree suspension, a deportation programme, bills submitted by the executive to Congress, and a constitutional amendment package submitted by the executive to the Senate. No third-party conduct is scored against Chile. The amendment package is a **proposal that Congress has not passed**; it is scored under anticipatory awareness and values alignment as evidence about how the government approaches rights, not as an enacted harm.

## Key Findings

- Chile's government withdrew a regularisation pathway from about 182,000 people **after** they had voluntarily identified themselves to the state. MercoPress reported on 31 March 2026 that the migration service head said: "We are not going to carry out a mass regularization like the one proposed under the Boric government." People who trusted a state process were left exposed by its cancellation.
- Human Rights Watch asked Chile's Congress on 1 September 2026 to reject eight constitutional amendments. The package would let the president declare a "public security" emergency lasting up to 240 days without congressional approval, and suspend freedom of movement and assembly. HRW called it "a dangerous power grab".
- NPR reported on 23 May 2026 that Chile sent two bills to Congress that "would criminalise illegal entry, as well as limit immigrants' access to social security benefits." Cutting a named group out of social security is the clearest equity finding in this assessment.
- Chile's service delivery is genuinely strong and is scored that way. The GES/AUGE explicit health guarantees set legally binding maximum waiting times and publish compliance data — anchor 4 for responsiveness. Action is the highest-scoring dimension at 3.4 of 5.
- A claimed 2026 United Nations finding against Chile turned out to be from 2023 and was excluded. No tier-4-or-above source for the 2026 conduct was found, so the band change is filed as unproven and routed for human review.

## Strongest Dimensions

**Action (3.4)** and **Awareness (3.2)**. Chile runs real, published, legally binding service standards through the GES/AUGE health guarantees, and a long-running disaggregated household survey (CASEN) that genuinely informs programme design. The 2025 pension reform moved resources toward the lowest-income elderly, which is a documented reallocation toward higher need rather than a historical-pattern allocation.

## Weakest Dimensions

**Empathy (2.4)**, **Equity (2.4)** and **Integrity (2.4)**. All three fall on the same body of evidence. A named population of roughly 336,000 people is the target of an announced expulsion programme; a regularisation pathway was withdrawn from 182,000 who had already registered; and bills before Congress would cut migrants out of social security. Integrity falls specifically on resilience of care: one change of government reversed the commitment, which is the anchor-2 description almost exactly.

## Evidence Gaps

- No tier-4-or-above source (UN body, treaty body, court or international organisation) was located for Chile's 2026 conduct. The 2023 material initially surfaced was excluded. This is the single largest gap and is the reason the band change is unproven.
- No evidence was found either way on Boundaries subdimension B1 (self-sustainability of frontline public-service staff). B1 is scored 3 on the general functioning of Chilean public administration, not on specific evidence.
- The eight constitutional amendments had not been voted on as of the assessment date. Their fate is unknown and could move Integrity and Awareness in either direction.
- No in-window community or migrant testimony was located. Empathy scores rest on documented government decisions, not on the accounts of those affected.

## Disclosure — what this score measures

This score movement measures **both** conduct and calibration, and the two must not be blurred.

The conduct part is real, documented and attributed to the Chilean state: the regularisation freeze, the deportation flights, the expulsion target, the two bills, and the constitutional amendment package. That is misconduct evidence in the ordinary sense.

The calibration part is that the published 62.5 rested on a near-uniform seed vector (seven of eight dimensions at exactly 3.5) that had never been individually evidenced. Replacing a seed with an assessment produces movement even where nothing has changed. A reviewer should not read the full -20.0 as a measure of new Chilean harm.

No subdimension in this assessment was scored below 2 on absence of disclosure alone. Where evidence was absent (B1), the score is 3 on general institutional functioning and the gap is stated.

## Recommendation

**Filed as `flag-for-review`, not as a recommended downgrade.** Two separate reasons, both procedural rather than doubts about the underlying facts:

1. The band-crossing evidence test failed. There is no tier-4-or-above within-period source. The facts are well corroborated at tiers 2 and 3, but the benchmark's own rule for moving an entity across a band boundary is not met.
2. The magnitude (-20.0) mixes conduct with the replacement of a near-uniform seed vector. An isolated 20-point drop presented as pure conduct would misdescribe what happened.

**Recommended remedy:** treat Chile as part of a cohort review of never-individually-assessed Established-band countries carrying near-uniform 3.5 vectors, rather than as a one-off move. If the founder applies the figure anyway, the applied record must state that part of the movement replaces a seed and that no tier-4 source supports the band change.
