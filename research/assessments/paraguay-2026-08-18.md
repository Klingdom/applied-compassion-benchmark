## Why this entity was assessed

The scanner flagged Paraguay on the "Paraguay Duele" marches in Asunción on 2026-08-14, three years into President Santiago Peña's term, at which police blocked one of the two marches. Paraguay has never been individually assessed (`last_assessed: null`), so this is a first full assessment.

## Evidence-date and attribution checks

**Dates verified.** Infobae and Última Hora both date the marches to 2026-08-14, inside the window. The scanner listed one undated source (telesurtv.net); it was disregarded, and a second dated source (Última Hora) was substituted so the finding rests on two independently dated reports.

**Attribution.** The Paraguayan National Police is a national state agency, so blocking the educators' and government workers' march is the entity's own conduct. **Proportionality is recorded:** one of two marches was blocked, the demonstrators then rallied on the spot, and no violence, injuries or arrests were reported. This is a restriction on assembly, not a lethal crackdown, and it is scored accordingly.

The structural finding — Law 7363/24 restricting civil-society organisations — is legislation enacted by the Paraguayan state and is scored in full.

### Analysis

The assessed composite is 20.6 against a published 25.0 — a fall of 4.4 points, below the 5.0-point filing trigger, with no band change. The published score is confirmed.

The evidence is consistent and moderate rather than dramatic. Paraguay enacted Law 7363/24 in November 2024, widely described as an anti-NGO law; 38 civil-society organisations have petitioned the Supreme Court to strike it down. Amnesty International's March 2026 regional briefing places Paraguay among the states tightening controls on civil society, and records that the campaigns preceding these laws used racist and misogynistic rhetoric, producing fear and self-censorship. Indigenous communities continue to lose land with weak enforcement of collective rights. Multidimensional poverty stands at 15.67% and monetary poverty at 20.1%.

Against that, Paraguay's institutions function. The Supreme Court is available to civil society and is being used. Elections are held and power has transferred. National statistics on multidimensional poverty are produced and published, which is why A1 scores 3 rather than 2.

**A calibration note, recorded as a note and not as a proposal.** Applying the absence-of-disclosure convention rigorously — score 2 where an institution simply does not publish — lands Paraguay at 20.6, which is 0.6 points above the Critical boundary and below Guatemala (23.4). Paraguay's institutional record does not obviously belong below Guatemala's. This is a cohort-calibration question about how the Latin-America band was set, not a reason to move Paraguay on its own. Per the anti-false-positive rule, it is recorded here for coordinator-level review rather than encoded as a one-off score change.

## Key Findings

- Why it matters: Paraguay made it harder for the groups that help its poorest people to operate. Law 7363/24, enacted in November 2024, imposes controls on civil-society organisations that Amnesty International says directly affect their ability to support communities.
- Thirty-eight civil-society organisations have asked Paraguay's Supreme Court to block the law. That the court is available to them, and being used, is itself evidence that Paraguay's institutions still function.
- On 14 August 2026 workers, teachers and peasant groups marched in Asunción under the banner "Paraguay Duele" — "Paraguay hurts". Police blocked one of the two marches. No violence, injuries or arrests were reported.
- Poverty is measured and published: 15.67% of Paraguayans face multidimensional poverty, 20.1% are below the monetary poverty line and 4.1% are in extreme poverty. Measuring it is a genuine strength; acting on it is not evidenced.
- The score is confirmed at 25.0. The assessed value of 20.6 is 4.4 points lower, inside the tolerance for a desk assessment, so no change is proposed.
