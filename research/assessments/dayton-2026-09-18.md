## Why this entity was assessed

The scanner flagged Dayton (priority 45, T2) on a 56,000-dollar settlement, approved by the city commission in September 2026, of a lawsuit over the city's enforcement of a permit requirement against a volunteer arrested for handing food to a homeless man. A federal judge had already barred the city from enforcing the requirement. Dayton has never been individually assessed.

## Evidence-date, provenance, attribution and screening checks

**ATTRIBUTION: THIS IS CITY CONDUCT, PROPERLY SCORED.** Unlike three other rows in this cycle, there is no attribution problem. The ordinance is a City of Dayton ordinance. The arresting officer was a Dayton police officer enforcing it. The appeal was filed by the city. The settlement was approved by the Dayton city commission. Every scored act belongs to the entity under assessment.

**THE SEQUENCE, ESTABLISHED FROM SOURCES.** For 26 years Dayton required a permit to distribute food, clothing or toiletries in a public place in the central area of the city, with a 50-dollar fee per event and a 250-dollar security deposit for organisations with prior violations. On 7 April 2024 a Dayton officer ordered volunteers from Nourish Our Neighbors to stop; when volunteer Mitchell West handed a man a burrito he was handcuffed and detained for more than 30 minutes. No charges were brought. The nonprofit sued in November 2024. In June 2026 Judge Thomas Rose of the Southern District of Ohio held the ordinance unconstitutional under the First Amendment and barred enforcement. The city appealed. In September 2026 the city commission approved a settlement of 56,365.62 dollars covering the nonprofit's attorney fees and costs. The city declined to comment.

## Key Findings

- Dayton's score falls 5.9 points, from 35.9 to 30.0 out of 100, and a change proposal is filed. It stays in the Developing band.
- The city criminalised giving food to homeless people without a permit, and enforced it. On 7 April 2024 a Dayton officer handcuffed volunteer Mitchell West for handing a homeless man a burrito in Courthouse Square, and detained him for over 30 minutes. No charges were ever brought.
- A federal judge struck the ordinance down in June 2026 as an unconstitutional restriction on protected expression. Dayton appealed that ruling, then dropped the appeal and paid 56,365.62 dollars in September 2026. It declined to comment.
- Every dollar of that money went to the nonprofit's lawyers. Nothing went to the man who was handcuffed, and nothing went to the homeless people who went unfed for the two years the case took. That is the benchmark's lowest anchor for repair, word for word.
- The worst mark is on Accountability, which drops from 2.4 to 1.6 out of 5. A court told the city it had caused harm and the city still has not said so.
- Unlike three other entities assessed tonight, there is no question of blaming someone else here. The ordinance, the officer, the appeal and the settlement were all Dayton's.

- The federal court order itself was not obtained. Reporting of it was used, so no tier 4 or better source is in hand. Confidence is medium for that reason.
- The ruling date differs between sources: Pacific Legal Foundation says 23 June 2026, WYSO and the Dayton Daily News say 24 June 2026. Not resolved; it does not affect any score.
- Two of the four moving subdimensions lean on Pacific Legal Foundation material. Pacific Legal is the nonprofit's own counsel and therefore an interested party, tier 1. Its factual claims used here (arrest date, detention length, ruling holding) are corroborated by WYSO and the Dayton Daily News.
- No city explanation of the ordinance's original public purpose was located beyond the judge's characterisation.
- Dayton has never been individually assessed; the five unmoved dimensions are grid expansions of a placeholder vector.

## Scanner and source corrections

- The scan says '$56,000'. The Dayton Daily News reports the commission approved 56,365.62 dollars. The round figure comes from the nonprofit's lawyers' description.
- The scan says the settlement was 'over its enforcement of a permit requirement against a resident arrested for distributing food'. Precisely: the volunteer was Mitchell West, he handed over a single burrito, and no charges were brought.
- The scan does not record that the city appealed the ruling before settling. The appeal is material, because it is the act that makes anchor 1 rather than anchor 2 correct on Harm Acknowledgment.
- The scan does not record that the settlement covers attorney fees and costs only. That fact is what puts Reparative Action at anchor 1.
